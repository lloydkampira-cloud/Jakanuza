A Debt Called Monday

By Lloyd Kampira

Life can be unpredictable, especially when responsibilities are waiting and opportunities are hard to find.

Ajijo lay on the old mattress, staring at the cracked ceiling. Beside him, his wife turned and sighed deeply. The baby slept between them, wrapped in a thin chitenje.

“Ajijo,” she said, her voice tired, “there is no food in the house. The baby needs pampers. Even body Vaseline is finished. And the village bank wants their money , K85,000. They said no more excuses.”

Ajijo swallowed hard. His stomach was already empty, but the weight in his chest was heavier.

“I will go to Limbe today,” he said softly. “I don’t know where to start, but I will hustle. I promise I will bring something.”

His wife said nothing. She just turned away.

Ajijo woke up before sunrise and walked out on an empty stomach. From Bisiyele, the road to Limbe felt longer than usual. His mind was full of promises he was not sure he could keep.

In Limbe, he went straight to Man Kho, the shoes wholesaler. Man Kho looked at him with cold eyes.

“You again?” Man Kho said. “last time you didn’t bring enough money. If you do that today, Ajijo, our relationship is finished.”

Ajijo nodded quickly. “I understand, bwana. Today will be better.”

Man Kho handed him shoes anyway. Commission business, as usual.

Ajijo stood around Chichiri Shoprite, opposite Kamuzu Stadium, together with three other friends. They spread out their shoes and waited for customers. Minutes passed. Hours passed. No sales. People just looked and walked away.

Then, from nowhere, a Blantyre City Council and police Land Rover arrived.

“Run!” someone shouted.

But it was too late.

They were arrested for vending in a restricted area and taken to the police cells. The cold floor welcomed them. That night was long and dark.

One by one, Ajijo’s three friends were released. They had managed to pay something to the police officers. Ajijo had nothing. He had sold nothing the whole day.

It was Friday night. The police told him, “You will wait until Monday. Courts don’t work on weekends.”

Ajijo sat alone, hungry, ashamed, and afraid.

On Monday morning, he was taken to court. His heart was beating fast. Then, from nowhere, he saw a familiar face.

Man Kho.

Without many words, Man Kho paid K150,000 kwacha. Ajijo was released.

As they walked out, Man Kho spoke calmly.

“That money is not a gift amwene, it's a loan. You will pay it back.”

Ajijo nodded again, tears in his eyes.

“Thank you, bwana. I will pay you back. You have saved me.”

He rushed home, hopeful to see his wife and baby.

But the house was empty.

Mr. Wasili, the landlord, stood at the door with crossed arms.

“Ajijo, today is the twelfth. Every month, excuses. I am tired. Take your things and go.”

Ajijo tried to speak, but the door was already closed.

He stood outside with nothing... no wife, no baby, no house, a loan on his back, hunger in his stomach, and nowhere to go.

Ajijo looked down the road, not knowing which direction his life would take next
